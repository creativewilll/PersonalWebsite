# Why B2B Buyers Ask ChatGPT Before They Visit Your SaaS Homepage

**B2B buyers ask ChatGPT before they visit a SaaS website because they want one prompt to compare vendors, and the homepage is a monologue that loads after that comparison.** G2's April 15, 2026 release, on a March 2026 survey of 1,076 B2B software buyers, says 51% now start software research in an AI chatbot more often than Google, up from 29% in April 2025 ([G2 press release](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/new-g2-research-half-of-b2b-software-buyers-now-start-their-research-with-ai-chatbots-302742807.html)).

I'm William Spurlock, founder of Spurlock Studios LLC. I work as an AI Systems Architect and Fractional AI CTO. The receipts I will stand on are 600+ automations built and 500+ live, 20,000+ hours architecting agentic systems, and 35,000+ hours saved for clients. I will not invent a SaaS logo, a demo count, or a revenue line for this post.

This pillar owns one query: why do B2B buyers ask ChatGPT before visiting a SaaS website? The pages that earn the citation, review profiles, comparison tables, docs, and a public security page, are already in [how SaaS brands get cited in the AI answers their buyers already trust](/blog/how-saas-brands-get-cited-in-the-ai-answers-their-buyers-already-trust). I am not rewriting that stack. The concentration math, one name taking most of the mentions, is in [why ChatGPT recommends one brand and ignores the rest](/blog/why-chatgpt-recommends-one-brand-and-ignores-the-rest). This post is the room order. Chat first. Homepage second, if they come at all.

## Why do B2B buyers ask ChatGPT before visiting a SaaS website?

**They ask ChatGPT first because the job is a comparison, and a homepage introduces one company.** In that same April 15, 2026 G2 release, comparing vendor strengths and weaknesses is the number-one chatbot use in software research, at 41%, ahead of basic product research, vendor identification, and use case validation. ChatGPT is the chatbot they open for that research: 63% in the same release. A separate 41% in that release use Deep Research tools regularly. Those are two different cuts. I will not blend them into one "41% of everything."

OpenAI's help center says ChatGPT can search the web and answer with links, so the buyer does not have to open a separate search engine for a current reply. The same page says search citations can be incomplete, outdated, or incorrect, and that the buyer should open the cited source ([ChatGPT search](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/9237897-chatgpt-search)). The buyer still receives names before your domain loads, even when those names are wrong.

G2's report page states the order in a sentence I keep: buyers one-shot a prompt and get a consideration set before they have visited a single vendor website ([Answer Economy report](https://learn.g2.com/g2-2026-ai-search-insight-report)). Tim Sanders, writing the same day on G2's newsroom, adds the rank: AI chatbots are the top source influencing buyer shortlists, ahead of review sites, analyst firms, and vendor websites ([G2 newsroom, April 15, 2026](https://company.g2.com/news/g2-research-the-answer-economy)). Your homepage is a vendor website. It is in the list of sources that are not first.

They have not quit Google. Sanders reports that 61% use AI search alongside Google. That split matters. Half the surveyed buyers start in the chatbot more often. A large share still use both. The homepage is not competing with "search is dead." It is competing with a reply that already named someone else.

The three compressions Sanders names are a useful picture, and I will not dress them up:

1. The yellow pages put a market in one book.
2. Google put it on the first page of results.
3. The chatbot puts it in one reply.

I think the homepage team still writes for compression two. The buyer already lives in compression three. A prettier hero does not move you from the book to the reply. It decorates a room the buyer enters after the reply, or skips.

Only 3% of buyers in that G2 release say AI chatbots have not meaningfully changed their research habits. I do not treat the rest of that survey as a law of nature. It is one March 2026 survey of 1,076 buyers. It is enough to stop treating the homepage as the front door.

## What has a B2B buyer already decided before the SaaS homepage loads?

**A lot of them have already switched the vendor they meant to buy, and some of them are about to meet a name they had never heard.** The April 15, 2026 G2 release says 69% chose a different software vendor than they initially planned, based on chatbot guidance, and 33% purchased from a vendor they were not familiar with. 85% think more highly of a vendor an AI chatbot mentions. Being left out is not a neutral blank. The named vendors got a lift the homepage never had a chance to match.

Gartner published the human half on May 20, 2026. In a survey of 645 B2B buyers run from August through September 2025, 45% used generative AI primarily to gather vendor and product information, and they used an average of seven information sources on a recent purchase. 69% prefer to validate AI-generated insights with a sales rep. 51% say they are more likely to encounter misleading information from generative AI, while 49% say that about a sales rep ([Gartner, May 20, 2026](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-05-20-gartner-survey-finds-sixty-nine-percent-of-b-two-b-buyers-turn-to-sales-reps-to-validate-ai-generated-insights)).

I read those two studies side by side. G2 says the chatbot changes the vendor. Gartner says the buyer still wants a person to check the chatbot, and that the buyer is slightly more worried about being misled by the model than by the rep. The homepage sits in the gap. It is not the first impression, and it is not the signature. It is the page they open when they want to see whether the reply survives contact with your own words.

G2's later buyer report, a June 2026 survey of 1,038 B2B decision-makers, says the first list sticks. More than 80% had sourced software recommendations from an AI chatbot in the prior two years. Among those buyers, half said AI had its greatest influence during shortlisting and evaluation. Buyers who sourced recommendations from chatbots bought from their initial shortlist in at least three of their last five purchases at 80%, compared with 65% of buyers who did not ([G2 2026 Buyer Behavior Report](https://sell.g2.com/2026-buyer-behavior-report)). I will not turn 80% versus 65% into "you can never break in later." G2's word is that the first list has a structural advantage. That is the stake for a homepage that only introduces you.

Four things I assume are already set when a SaaS homepage finally loads:

- The category words, in the buyer's phrasing, not the tagline.
- Two or three other product names, spelled the way the reply spelled them.
- A trust worry. G2 says 64% encounter inaccurate chatbot recommendations often or very often.
- A plan to ask a person. Gartner's 69% did not fire the sales team. They moved the sales team to the check.

I do not know which two names your category produces. I will not invent them. Put the prompt you hear on calls into the blank, run it, and write the names down. If you skip that and redesign the hero from a brand workshop, you are decorating a room for a guest who already ate.

## Why does a SaaS homepage lose the first buying question?

**It loses because the first question is "which of these fits this constraint," and the homepage answers "here is our story."** Sanders is explicit that chatbots outrank vendor websites as a shortlist influence. The homepage can still be beautiful and still be the wrong instrument for that question.

The mismatch is concrete. A buyer prompt sounds like this: "best [category] for a [team size] that needs [SSO or a named security report] and does not need [the thing you refuse]." I leave the brackets empty on purpose. Filling them with a fake customer would be a story I do not have. Your prompt has the blanks filled. Your homepage, in my experience of reading them, fills a different blank: a metaphor, a customer logo row, and a button that says book a demo.

That button is not useless. It is late. By the time someone is willing to book, G2's March survey says the chatbot may already have swapped the vendor (69%) or introduced a stranger (33%). The button records the arrival. It does not record the swap.

What I cut from the hero before I touch color:

1. A coined category that no buyer types.
2. A logo row that does not name the constraint those companies had.
3. A demo button with no sentence about who should not book.
4. A security badge with no scope, no date, and no path to the public page.

The pages that can win the prompt are not this hero. They are the comparison, the docs, the security page, and the review profile, which is the work in the citation post I linked above. I am opinionated about the sequence: if the hero is the only page you rewrite this month, you polished the second room. Do the prompt log first, then the page the citation actually quotes.

71% of the March 2026 buyers rely on an AI chatbot somewhere in software research, up from 60% seven months earlier, in the April 15 release. 53% say that chatbot research feels more productive than traditional search, up from 36% over the same gap. Productivity is their word for "I got a list without opening you." Your homepage cannot out-produce a list it is not in.

A session report will not show you the shortlist.** I do not have an official OpenAI or Google figure, read on October 8, 2026, for what share of ChatGPT referrals arrive as direct traffic. I will not invent that share. If your analytics pie has no ChatGPT slice, that is not proof the chat never happened. It is proof the pie is the wrong instrument.

Gartner already told you the purchase used an average of seven information sources. The homepage is at most one of them. The other six never have to request your HTML.

How I read a week of rows:

- Absent in the prompt, and the demos never mention a rival: you are being found somewhere else. Do not congratulate the homepage. Find the actual source.
- Absent in the prompt, and the demos quote a rival's feature: the chat is upstream. The homepage is a confirmation page for a list you are not on.
- Named, but the cited URL is a review site or a docs page: good. Send the homepage visitor to that same URL in one click. Do not make them hunt.
- Named, but a fact is wrong: fix the public sentence the citation can quote. Gartner's buyers already suspect generative AI of being the more misleading source (51% versus 49% for a rep). Your page is the correction, or it is another vague paragraph.

What I will not count as evidence:

4. A dollar loss for missing the list. Neither G2 nor Gartner published your pipeline.

## What should a SaaS homepage confirm once ChatGPT already wrote the shortlist?

**Confirm the constraint, the skip, and the human check. Stop introducing the company in the first screen.** The June 2026 G2 survey says evaluation is now the longest stage for 40% of buyers, ahead of research at 36% and decision at 22%. After a vendor is selected, IT security review is the top delay at 39%, and 50% for enterprise buyers. A homepage still selling discovery is late twice. Late to the shortlist, and useless in the stall that happens after the shortlist.

The June survey also splits shortlist influence differently from the March release. Review sites lead at 38%. AI chatbots are next at 37%. March, via Sanders, called chatbots the top shortlist source, ahead of review sites and vendor websites. I believe both reports. I do not average them into a fake ranking. The operating conclusion is narrower: in both cuts, the vendor website is not the thing writing the list. Reviews and the chatbot are. The homepage should point at the proof those two already use.

Five lines I want on the first screen, in this order:

1. **Category in the buyer's words.** If the prompt says "contract review" and the hero says "agreement intelligence," you made the model translate. It may not.
2. **Who it is for, with one limit.** Headcount, stack, or a system you require. "Teams of all sizes" is not a limit.
3. **Who it is not for.** One skip. Comparison prompts need a skip or they invent one.
4. **The security or data fact a reviewer will ask.** Name the control and link the public page. The June report says this is where the deal waits.
5. **What the sales call will confirm.** Gartner's 69% want a rep to validate the AI. Tell them the three questions the call is for. Do not make the call re-deliver the hero.

The order I would ship, and I would not reverse it:

1. This week's prompt log, five real buyer wordings, search on, seats written down.
2. The first screen rewritten to the five lines above, using words from those prompts.
3. The citation pages, if the log shows you absent or cited on the wrong URL. That build is the August post, not a second copy here.
4. A sales note: ask every demo which names were already on the list. Gartner put the rep on validation duty. Use the rep as a sensor.

I think a brand story still belongs on the site. It does not belong in the first screen. Put it under the confirmation, for the buyer who already matched the constraint and wants the people. If you lead with the people, you answered a question the chatbot did not ask.

## FAQ

**The short answers below use the same G2 and Gartner figures as the sections above. I am not adding a new study here.**

### Do B2B buyers still open the homepage after ChatGPT names a vendor?

**Some do. The homepage is no longer the start.** G2's report says the chatbot returns a consideration set before a buyer has visited a single vendor website, and Sanders writes that discovery and evaluation are unfolding in the AI reply, not on your site. When they do open you, Gartner says they are often checking a generative answer they do not fully trust. Treat the visit as a confirmation, not an introduction.

### Is ChatGPT the chatbot B2B software buyers use most?

**Yes, in G2's March 2026 survey. ChatGPT is the dominant chatbot for B2B software research at 63%.** That is why this post names ChatGPT and not a generic "AI tool." The same release says the field is moving, so I would still log a second tool if your buyers mention one. Do not let 63% talk you into ignoring a tool your own calls keep naming.

### Does a demo request mean the buyer already asked an AI chatbot?

**A demo request does not prove a ChatGPT session. The base rate says you should ask.** In G2's June 2026 survey, more than 80% had sourced software recommendations from an AI chatbot in the prior two years. On the call, ask which names were already listed. If you only record "demo booked," you will credit the homepage for a decision the chat already shaped.

### Should I rewrite the SaaS homepage or the comparison page first?

**Run the prompt log first, then rewrite the page the reply actually cites.** If the citation is a comparison, a docs URL, or a review profile, start there. The homepage confirmation is the second edit, for the people who arrive to check the list. The full citation stack is in [how SaaS brands get cited](/blog/how-saas-brands-get-cited-in-the-ai-answers-their-buyers-already-trust). A homepage-only sprint repeats the page that lost the first question.

### Why do buyers still want a sales rep after ChatGPT answers?

**Because they want the reply checked, not because the chatbot failed to exist.** Gartner's May 20, 2026 release says 69% prefer to validate AI-generated insights with a sales rep, and 51% think they are more likely to meet misleading information from generative AI than from a rep (49%). The call is a verification step. Brief the rep with the prompt log, not with a tour of the hero.

### Can ChatGPT send a buyer to a SaaS vendor they have never heard of?

**Yes. One in three buyers in G2's March 2026 survey purchased from a vendor they were not familiar with, after chatbot guidance.** Fame on your homepage is not the gate. A quotable constraint, a review citation, and a public page the model can point at are the gate. 85% of those buyers also think more highly of a vendor the chatbot names, so a stranger with a clean mention starts ahead of a known brand that was left off the list.

### Do review citations matter if the buyer never starts on my homepage?

**They matter more, not less.** The April 15, 2026 G2 release says 45% of buyers call a software-review citation the most confidence-inspiring signal in an AI answer, and review sites are the only source besides chatbots that gains influence deeper in, from 40% at discovery to 47% at retention. The June survey puts review sites at 38% of shortlist influence and chatbots at 37%. If the buyer never sees your hero, the review profile is one of the pages the reply can still quote.

### How often should I rerun the prompt buyers type into ChatGPT?

**I rerun the same five prompts weekly, and I do not call one week a market.** OpenAI says citations can be incomplete or wrong, and G2 says 64% of buyers hit inaccurate recommendations often or very often. A weekly row catches a swapped name or a bad fact. The day-by-day method is the [one-week shortlist audit](/blog/how-to-audit-a-chatgpt-shortlist-in-a-week). Keep the homepage sentence in the same row so you can see whether the first screen still matches the reply.

### Does winning the ChatGPT shortlist mean the homepage can stay a brand story?

**No. A mention gets you into the check. It does not finish the check.** June 2026 buyers who sourced from chatbots stuck with their initial shortlist more often (80% versus 65% in at least three of the last five purchases), and evaluation is the longest stage for 40% of buyers. If you won the reply and your first screen is still a slogan, you can lose the visit that was supposed to confirm you. Lead with the constraint the prompt already asked.
